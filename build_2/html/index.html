BLOCKHEAD
On 28th February 6:15 AM UTC, American and Israeli forces launched the first strikes of Operation Epic Fury and Operation Roaring Lion.
Hundreds were killed on the first day as munitions rained down on Iran. Among the dead was Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, who had ruled the Islamic republic without reprieve since 1989.
In the days that followed, defence officials celebrated the assassination as a intelligence coup. The strike had been planned for years, and it couldn't be more perfect.
However, not everyone was caught unawares. 8,000 miles away in a sleepy London suburb, a different type of killing had taken place. One hour before the strike, on Amazon's EU-West-2 data center, Polymarket's CLOB servers was flooded with a flurry of activity.
A small group of wallets, many of them with no trading history, had placed well-timed bets on an Iran war starting on the 28th, earning them profits in worth approximately $6 million in total.
One of the biggest winners was Vivaldi007. The account made US$700,000 by correctly predicting the day of the strike, the successful assasination of Khamenei, as well as Israel's close participation.
Despite its exceptional foresight, Vivaldi007 had no interest in making a second set of wagers. A total of USD$400,000 was transferred out on the same day, while the remainng would be transferred out on the 3rd March.
Like so many of its peers who had made similar bets on Iran, the account has been mostly inactive since.

Vivaldi007 was just one of the many users on Polymarket, an online exchange where users can place bets on the likelihood of future events.
The operations are disarmingly simple. Every market poses a binary question that can be answered YES / NO. Every share of YES/NO is priced according to the probability of the event: $0 for 0% and $1 for 100%.
If you own 100,000 shares of an improbable event bought at $0.10 each, you could make $90,000 when the event came true. If you own 100,000 shares of a highly-probable event bought at $0.98 each, your profits would be considerably lower at $2,000. Profits are commensurate with the risks involved.
Explainer: How much can $1,000 get you on Polymarket?
Despite its relative simplicity, prediction markets like Polymarket have a long and illustrious track record. They've been successful in forecasting everything from Federal reserve rates to election results. The non-profit Iowa Electronic markets—has been in operation since the 1980s. They've beaten traditional polls74% of the time.
However, the surging popularity of profit-driven platforms like Polymarket and Kalshi has attracted a storm of controversy. Many critics call them a front for unregulated online gambling. Media outlets from the WSJ to More Perfect Union have investigated the companies for preying on the user's financial vulnerability.
Most finance professionals are more circumspect. Simon, a pro forecaster with the RAND corporation, argues they could provide 'a lot of value' in areas with poor information and high uncertainty.
"If we were flying through Dubai, and the conflict kicks off again. There's at least some value in knowing there's a 20% chance flights will be cancelled, and that's important for a multi milllion dollar industry."
Jon Reiter, Chief Data Scientist of blockchain analytics firm ChainArgos, also believes in their utility to the markets. "Businesses need to be aware of upcoming business events like tax rates, and elections affect tax rates and tax rates affeect what country you build your factory in," he said "it's kind of a simple example but it's real."
"I mean, more information about the world is always an unmitigated social good"
The only catch is when such information is illegal or classified. In such cases, prediction betting is likely to have a corrosive effect on public institutions and trust, said Jon. The volumes on Polymarket might be smaller than stocks or commodities, but they have a outsized impact on public perception.
"$10,000 made by a cabinet minister betting on military deployments has much more of a negative effect than $10,000 overcharged on the highway construction contracts, for example," he said.
Unfortunately, the DeFi structure of Polymarket—anonymous to a fault—makes it difficult to distinguish corruption from prediction. Since the start of 2026, four men have been arrested for trading on confidential information on Polymarket. They include a US special forces operator who traded on Maduro's capture, a Google engineer who traded on the top search terms and two IDF officers who traded on military strikes in the Middle East.
Polymarket see the arrests as a affirmation of 'market integrity', but the numbers tell a different story. According to on-chain data examined by BLOCKHEAD Research, large flows of anomlaous activity around Iran.

For some, the timing seemed either suspect or extremely lucky. 0x283492, an account with no prior history of trading, took a long position less than 2 hours before the strikes began.
By 9AM, its $18,700 investment would be worth $98,700, a 500% return capital. All of the money would be cashed out between 6-7pm on the same day.
Another account flagged for suspicious activity is Dicedicedice. Between 1-2 AM on the morning of the 28th February, the account bought 149,995 YES Shares at $0.20 per share.

On-chain data from Polygonscan and Polywatcher reveal just how new it was. Dicedicedice was created just one day before the strike on 27th February. It was funded at 2 minutes past midnight on the 28th February, just 5 minutes before it made its massive bet on the Iran war.
Several of the accounts on Polymarket are also closely coordinated and connected.

The anonymity of onchain transactions make it difficult to ascertain a true insider, but the pattern of suspicious behaviour is equally difficult to dismiss. 64 of the 79 wallets highlighted by BLOCKHEAD Research were less than 2 months old. 71 of them have been completely inactive since their wins on the 28th of February.
At least 6 of the Polymarket acccounts have been deleted at the time of writing. Their display names are now listed as 'anon'—the default assigned to deleted wallets.
Jon, who specialises in blockchain investigations, finds their funding patterns odd. Three of the wallets (0x45647, 0x2584d, 0x45647) were all using the same 'somewhat obscure' bridge to fund their Polymarket accounts.
"This does not mean they are the same person. It doesn't even prove they are all related. but Polymarket is a casino. think of this as catching 3 "unrelated" card cheats at your casino and they all have receipts for the same motorway rest stop 500km away in their pockets. it is suspicious," he explained.
Unusual pattern are also present in the volume and timing of many Iran markets. BLOCKHEAD Research analysed the market's liquidity in the six hours before strikes began. In three separate markets, trading had spiked sharply between 5-6AM, an hour before the first strikes began.
The spike was highly directional. Most of the traffic came from transactions buying YES shares for 'Will the US Strike Iran by 28th February?" "Will the US Strike Iran by 31 March?" and "Will the US next strike Iran on February 28, 2026 (ET)?".

Accounting for the mystery of the 5AM surge is difficult. Mantic AI, a leading AI forecasting lab based in Oxfordshire, failed to pick up on the pre-strike signals which informed Polymarket traders. According to its Iran report, the first indications of conflict originating from market signals came at 6:17AM—2 minutes after the strikes had began.
Hannah Su, a freelance shipping analyst, has examined the AIS transponder data from the US Aircraft Carriers involved in the war. She says there is little open source information to directly indicate that a strike would be imminent. Neither the USS Abraham Lincoln nor the USS Gerald Ford had turned on its AIS transponder on the 28th—their last known locations were in the Straits of Malacca and off the coast of Morocco.

The Regulatory Pretzel
During the company's infancy, Polymarket CEO Shayne Coplan had celebrated the presence of insiders. In a CBS interview from 2024, Coplan claimed that insiders would 'bring a lot of benefits to the market.'
Kalshi's CEO made the same point, arguing for the relevance of 'nonmaterial nonpublic information'.
In recent months, however, the companies have changed their tune. The intense media scrutiny seemed to have spurred a policy reversal on the use of confidential information. In April, Polymarket has hired Chainanalysis—a leading blockchain analytics firm—to monitor its trading activities and to refine its 'detection methods.' It has also tapped Palantir and TWG AI to build what it calls a next generation 'Sports Integrity Platform'.

Left: Drew Cukor, TWG Global AI Lead and former Marine Corp Colonel responsible for the Pentagon's AI targeting system | Right: Shayne Coplan, Polymarket CEO

It remains to be seen if such regulation will be successful. Simon is optimistic because he believes that prediction markets have a commercial incentive to keep themselves corruption-free. He argues that it's not in Kalshi or Polymarket's self-interest to run 'weird scam markets where the outcome is known.'
The recent arrest of a Google Engineer for insider trading is a prime example. He said: "If I'm Google and I say to all employees let's go out there and use the company's information to bet... it would destroy any market in the long-term."
"Who's gonna want to bet on Google markets when the counterparty is Google? There's not like an endless supply of idiots who will be willing to give money to Google employees."
He also predicts that Polymarket and Kalshi will get better at preventing insider trading as their user base grows. "I think there will be protections in place. I think prediction markets will get better at not running markets that are just baked for insider trading; For example, will X candidate drop out of the election race at Y date."
Professor John Cheyne-Lippold, who researches digital futurity at the University of Michigan, does not agree. The idea that prediction markets can be effectively controlled and regulated violates the expansionary nature of these markets.
"The fact of capitalism is that capitalism needs to commodify and financialize and make everything a place where value can be created because of the historic law of depreciating returns."
"If you financialize everything, then you have to regulate everything, and it's really hard to regulate everything," he argues.
He is also skeptical of the idea that an equilibrium could be reached as users logically abandon markets which are unfair. In his ethnographic research on sports betting, Professor Lippold notes that many bettors believe in their own individual exceptionalism.
"A lot of people think they're smarter than Vegas, than the odds. There's this weird conceit where you think everyone's a sucker but me," he said. This self-delusion plays out on Polymarket as well, with users continuing to place bets on markets where they're clearly, evidently at a disadvantage.
"People are betting on how Love Island is going to end. How can you? That's insane. It already is finished, you know. For certain, that some people know that information."
At the Federal level, many critics are doubtful if the USA government even has the will to oversee Polymarket. For starters, Polymarket enjoys strong ties to the Trump administration. Donald Trump Junior is an advisor and investor to Polymarket thanks to his venture capital firm 1789 Capital. Trump himself has also praised prediction markets on Truth Social, promising that they will 'thrive' under his rule.
The body responsible for regulating prediction markets is the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). Its Chairman, Michael Selig, is a recent appointment who seems to spend more time defending the markets rather than investigating them.
The agency is currently mired in a tangle of lawsuits. Under Selig, the CFTC has sued Arizona, New Mexico, Connecticut, Kentucky and Illinois for attempting to ban prediction markets—arguing that it has exclusive regulatory jurisdiction.
Meanwhile, the CFTC is also being sued by the Chicago Mercantile Exchange for allowing Polymarket to sell 'perpetual futures' in violation of existing regulation. The CFTC dismissed CME's suit as 'lawfare' and mocked the exchange for being 'scared' of competition.

A litany of litigation since Chairman Michael Selig took office. Source: CFTC

In an interview with WIRED, Selig claimed the CFTC is using 'AI' to find hundreds, thousands of suspicious wallets. As of July, however, no one has been arrested or prosecuted in relation to Iran.
Blockchain experts like Jon do not believe technical hurdles are holding back law enforcement. His analysis indicated no 'mixers' or other obfuscation methods common to crypto. Many of the accounts were connected to Binance, which operates Know Your Customer verification for US consumers.
"It's either very simple or very professional, like a mule. Given the amounts, it is likely to be an amateur. This is something that law enforcement could unravel if it cared," he said.
The Future of Prediction
The future has become a gold rush. On 24 June, Mark Zuckerberg announced that Meta would develop its own prediction market called 'Arena'. He will join Coinbase, Smarkets, PredictIt, Crypto.com, Versus in an increasingly crowded landscape that offers odds on the world of tomorrow.
Alongside them, there's a thriving secondary market dedicated to insights, tools, bots and data that promises riches untold. Websites like Unusual Whales, Polygun, Poly Research and Robotics advertise their services by publishing a neverending stream of FOMO. They spotlight wallets of probable insiders. Wallets that struck big on Superbowl ads. Wallets with an uncanny record on what Trump would say next. Wallets dedicated to the weather in Seoul, London or Chicago.
Markets have grown rapidly in the past year. Especially in the Middle East.
The backlash is equally fervent, with nearly every major media outlet publishing some sort of exposé on the prediction industry. Early accusations of 'immorality' have given way to more detailed investigations on deceptive advertising or the unusual win rate of politics markets.
Even professional forecasters like Simon who've made careers from prediction are wary of the industry's association with gambling. In a utopian world, he says, prediction markets can serve as a useful financial instrument for companies to hedge risk—much like derivatives. For example, big companies could set up expected negative value bets to insure themselves from outcomes in the real world.
"If a company is going to be regulated out of existence when someone wins an election, they might bet on the person winning as a form of insurance," he suggests. "They might lose money when the politician loses, but they're going to be happy."
The negative externalities, however, are equally real.
"However, if instead it's just people just going and making degenerate bets on the outcome of some person says some word on some given day, and they're all losing money to the smarter traders... that's not ideal."
For Professor Cheyne-Lippold, however, there is a more interesting question. What is it about this particular moment in history that have made prediction markets so irresistible to so many? In his view, it is not just the promise of riches, but the diminishing returns from a precarious present.

Capitalism with monopoly operates under the law of depreciating returns, he argues. "If you have Little Caesars and Pizza Hut competing against each other, the profit margins are going to get thinner and thinner. Which is why you have to move abroad, import immigrant labour that you can pay very cheaply, or source materials from Sri Lanka instead of Detroit."
Prediction markets represent a temporal fork of capitalism's geographical expansion—a means of commodifying the near future. "If I can't make money from the present anymore, then I'm going to look to the temporary horizon of the future to make my money," he said.
Whatever the reason, the idea of trading on the future has proven impossible to resist, even under the threat of persecution. djijaij83jdo4jdlwjflsg. After making $50,000 on the start of the Iran war, he had transferred his winnings out of Polymarket at 11:40pm on the same day.
The user returned just three weeks later, to make a $67,592 bet on—"Iran-US ceasefire by April 7 and April 15". Once again, he was proven right. His investment yielded a 1000% return of $433,481.42.
